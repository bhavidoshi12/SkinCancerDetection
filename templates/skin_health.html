Understanding Skin Health & Lesions

Essential knowledge on skin cancer awareness, lesion self-monitoring, and the clinical ABCDE rule for early detection.

What is Skin Cancer?

Skin cancer is the abnormal and uncontrolled proliferation of skin cells, predominantly triggered by ultraviolet (UV) radiation from sunlight or tanning beds. It generally falls into two broad classifications:

Non-Melanoma Skin Cancer

Includes Basal Cell Carcinoma (BCC) and Squamous Cell Carcinoma (SCC). Highly prevalent, slow-growing, and exceptionally treatable when addressed early.

Melanoma

Arises in pigment-producing melanocytes. Although less frequent than BCC, it is more aggressive and prone to metastasize if not identified in its earliest, localized stages.

Clinical Self-Check

The ABCDE Rule of Melanoma Detection

Dermatologists recommend periodic skin self-examinations using the standard ABCDE criteria.

A

Asymmetry

One half of the mole or spot does not match the other half. Normal benign moles are typically symmetrical and circular or oval in shape.

B

Border Irregularity

The edges are irregular, ragged, notched, scalloped, or blurred, rather than smooth and sharply defined against surrounding skin.

C

Color Variation

The color is not uniform across the lesion. May display varying shades of tan, brown, black, or occasionally patches of pink, red, white, or slate-blue.

D

Diameter

The spot is larger than 6 millimeters across (approximately the size of a standard pencil eraser), although melanomas can sometimes be smaller when detected very early.

E

Evolving

The most critical indicator. Any mole or patch that changes in size, shape, elevation, color, or develops new sensations such as itching, crusting, or bleeding.

AI Screening vs. Professional Dermatological Diagnosis

It is essential to understand the distinction between preliminary digital screening and comprehensive medical diagnosis:

AI Screening Platforms

  • Statistical pattern recognition on 2D images.
  • Calculates mathematical similarity to training sets.
  • Identifies potential warning signs for prioritization.
  • Does not inspect tactile texture, depth, or pathology.

Clinical Dermatologist

  • Polarized dermoscopic multi-spectral optical evaluation.
  • Considers patient family history, UV exposure, and total body mole map.
  • Performs histological biopsy and microscopic pathology.
  • Definitive clinical diagnosis and treatment planning.

When Should You Consult a Doctor?

You should consult a board-certified dermatologist or primary healthcare practitioner immediately if you notice:

  • A mole that is growing quickly or noticeably altering its shape or border.
  • A sore, scab, or spot that fails to heal after 3 to 4 weeks.
  • A lesion that oozes, bleeds, itches, or becomes tender to touch.
  • The appearance of a new dark mark under a toenail or fingernail.
  • An "ugly duckling" lesion that looks markedly different from all other moles on your skin.
